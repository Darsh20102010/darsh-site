The next world superpower won't be decided in a boardroom or on a battlefield. It'll be decided in a semiconductor fab, somewhere no tourist has ever visited.

Here's a question nobody asked ten years ago: who controls the chips, controls the future? Today, it might be the most important question in geopolitics. Every large AI model you've heard of — GPT, Gemini, Claude — exists because someone had access to thousands of specialized processors running for months. Take those processors away, and the intelligence disappears. It's that brutally simple.

At the heart of this race are two chip types. GPUs (Graphics Processing Units), originally built to render video game explosions, turned out to be extraordinarily good at the parallel maths AI needs. TPUs (Tensor Processing Units), built by Google from scratch, go further — custom-designed purely for machine learning workloads, burning less power and moving faster. One chip was designed for gaming, while the other was designed to rule the world. (NVIDIA probably prefers you don't notice that distinction.)

This is where geopolitics bites. TSMC, the Taiwanese company that manufactures most of the world's advanced chips, sits on a small island at the centre of one of the tensest territorial disputes on earth. The US has imposed sweeping export controls blocking advanced semiconductors from reaching China. The EU is scrambling to build domestic capacity. India is offering subsidies to attract fabs. Everyone, suddenly, wants to be in the chip business.

The uncomfortable truth is that AI capability is currently gated by hardware access. A nation with brilliant mathematicians but no chips is like a racing driver with no car — talented, but going nowhere fast. Countries investing in domestic semiconductor ecosystems today aren't just protecting their tech industries; they're deciding whether they get a seat at the table when AI reshapes the global economy.

The intelligence race has a new prerequisite: build the chip, or buy it while you still can.
