The human brain has 100 billion neurons, each neuron connected to 10,000 other neurons. Sitting on your shoulders is the most complicated object in the known universe. And yet — we still don't fully understand it. Not even close. But here's a wild idea that researchers are actually exploring: what if instead of training AI on words and sentences, we trained it on the brain itself ? Think of the connectome as the brain's wiring diagram. Every neuron, every connection, every pathway — mapped out. Researchers are now using AI and the connectome — a map of neurons and their connections created from brain tissue — to predict the role of neurons in the living brain. It's like getting the blueprint of the world's most complicated building. Except the building also somehow produces consciousness. Casual.

The scale is almost absurd. The human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons. Princeton researchers recently mapped just one cubic millimeter of mouse brain tissue and found more than half a billion connections. A human connectome would make that look like a rough draft.

We already know that Graph Neural Networks — a type of AI that's really good at understanding connected systems — are powerful tools for this. Mapping the connectome of the human brain using structural or functional connectivity has become one of the most pervasive paradigms for neuroimaging analysis, with GNNs attracting broad interest for their established power in modeling complex networked data. In plain terms: if you treat every neuron as a node and every connection as an edge, the brain becomes a graph — and GNNs were practically built for this.

The idea of a "Connectome Transformer" takes this further. What if we used Self-Supervised Learning — the same technique behind large language models — but instead of predicting the next word, we predicted the next neural pathway? Pre-train on the brain's architecture. Build a Foundation Model for the human mind. It sounds like a sci-fi plot, and honestly it kind of is — except people are already working on pieces of it, which is somehow more exciting and terrifying at the same time.

This isn't just a cool science experiment. Princeton's Sebastian Seung compared mapping the human connectome to the Human Genome Project — which, two decades after completion, now lies at the forefront of medicine, personalizing treatments for cancer and helping doctors fine-tune doses of critical drugs. A brain foundation model could do something similar: predict how a specific brain will react to a drug, a neural implant, or a therapy before anything is ever administered. No more trial and error. No more "let's try this antidepressant and see what happens in six weeks." Which, as anyone who's been through that process knows, is a sentence nobody wants to hear.

The honest truth is that the human connectome is still being mapped. The tools are getting faster and cheaper every year, and a proposed $150 million national initiative aims to publish open-access, cell-level connectomes of multiple mammalian brains to accelerate this exact kind of research. The pieces are assembling — slowly, expensively, and with a lot of fMRI machines humming in the background.

The brain trained us. Maybe it's time we returned the favor.
