Nobody puts a data centre on a magazine cover. But without them, every AI model you've ever used would be a very expensive paperweight.

While the world debates whether AI will take our jobs or save the planet, a less glamorous story is unfolding in the background — one involving warehouse-sized buildings, industrial cooling systems, and enough electricity to power small cities. Data centres are the physical backbone of the AI revolution, and they are quietly becoming one of the most strategically important pieces of infrastructure on earth.

Here's the basic picture. Training a large AI model isn't something you do on a laptop, or even a powerful desktop. It requires thousands of GPUs running in parallel, communicating with each other at blistering speeds, for weeks or months at a stretch. Data centres are where all of that happens — massive facilities purpose-built to house, power, and cool the hardware that modern AI runs on. Think less "server room in a basement" and more "industrial complex with its own power substation."

The scale is staggering. A single large training run for a frontier model like GPT-4 is estimated to have consumed around 50 gigawatt-hours of electricity — roughly what 5,000 average American homes use in a year. And that's just one model, one time. With dozens of labs racing to train bigger, better models on tighter timelines, the cumulative energy demand is growing faster than most grids were designed to handle.

This is where things get genuinely complicated. Data centres need three things in abundance: power, water, and land. Power to run the chips. Water to cool them — modern facilities use millions of litres annually in evaporative cooling systems. And land, ideally near reliable energy sources, stable governments, and fast internet infrastructure. That specific combination isn't as common as you'd think, which is why data centre geography has become its own sub-field of geopolitical strategy.

Ireland hosts a disproportionate chunk of Europe's data infrastructure — so much so that its grid regulator has raised alarms about new approvals. Singapore, similarly, imposed a moratorium on new data centres for two years due to power constraints. Meanwhile, the American midwest and Scandinavian countries are booming — cheap land, cool climates that reduce cooling costs, and in the Nordic case, abundant renewable energy.

Environmental reckoning is arriving too. Microsoft, Google, and Amazon have all made net-zero pledges, but the math is uncomfortable. AI workloads are growing exponentially; renewable energy capacity is not. Nuclear power is quietly re-entering the conversation — Microsoft signed a deal to restart a unit at Three Mile Island specifically to power its AI infrastructure. The irony of powering artificial intelligence with nuclear fission is not lost on anyone.

What's easy to miss in all the excitement about AI capabilities is that every chatbot response, every image generated, every document summarised has a physical cost — measured in watts, litres, and square metres. The cloud, as the saying goes, is just someone else's building. And right now, those buildings are being built faster than we can reckon with what they demand. Data centres won't make the cover of a magazine. But they might just determine whether the AI revolution is sustainable — or spectacular and short-lived.
