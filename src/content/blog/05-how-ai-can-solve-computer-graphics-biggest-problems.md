> "It started this project seven years ago but couldn't make it — too expensive. Now AI makes it possible."

> — Timur Bekmambetov, Hollywood director (Wanted, Abraham Lincoln: Vampire Hunter), on using AI for his upcoming film Luria

If a visionary director can be stopped dead in his tracks — not by a lack of creativity, but by a rendering bill — something is broken. For decades, computer graphics has been the art form that rewards patience, deep pockets, and an almost masochistic tolerance for render times that finish approximately three days after you need them. AI is here to fix that.

Creating movie-quality computer graphics has traditionally been laborious and expensive — months or years of skilled artist time for effects that last seconds on screen. The difference that AI makes is mostly about time and cost — with AI, you can generate movie-quality special effects almost instantly.

Beyond cost, the technical bottlenecks are significant. Denoising, texture synthesis, physics simulation, and realistic facial animation each represent entire sub-fields that CG teams wrestle with daily. Topics like neural rendering, BRDF estimation, procedural modelling, mesh segmentation, and character animation are now being tackled through AI-driven, learning-based approaches, which, translated out of academic jargon, means machines are now doing the grunt work that used to require entire departments. And unlike those departments, they don't complain about overtime, go quiet in Slack, or mysteriously disappear before a deadline.

The impact of generative AI on 3D graphics and interactive techniques is profound, poised to democratize content creation and lower barriers to entry in creative industries — facilitating real-time, personalized experiences in gaming, architecture, and industrial design through procedural content generation and rapid prototyping. Tools like CLAY, developed at SIGGRAPH 2024, now generate complex 3D assets with accurate geometric features from simple text prompts — work that used to take weeks now takes minutes. No artist required. Cue the existential crisis for texture painters everywhere — right after they finish the asset they've been working on since Tuesday.

Meanwhile, AI-powered denoising has slashed rendering times by extraordinary margins, allowing studios to iterate faster than ever. Which is wonderful news, because "we're waiting on renders" has historically been the world's most expensive excuse.

Before anyone drafts a resignation letter, Oscar-winning VFX pioneer George Murphy offers a steadying perspective: "These tools are just new brushes in our paintbox — they allow us to push the boundaries of what's possible. But the artist's hand will always be there, guiding the story and making sure it resonates with the audience." AI doesn't dream. It doesn't feel like the weight of a story. It just executes — brilliantly, tirelessly, and without once asking for creative credit on IMDb.

The problems of computer graphics, such as cost, complexity, time, and accessibility, are real. AI isn't dissolving them so much as handing the keys to a wider room of creators. Everyone now has a chance to create something incredible - not just studios with nine-figure budgets, but also the filmmaker working alone at a laptop at 2 a.m., running on coffee and conviction. That's not a threat to the craft. That's the craft, finally growing up, and who knows, with the grace of AI, we may get an Avatar-like film done in a year.
